How to brief an AI chatbot for your website

A chatbot is only as good as what it is allowed to say. These are the five documents it needs before it can answer your customers well, and how to put each one together.

Most chatbots that disappoint were not badly built. They were badly briefed. Someone connected a language model to the website, pointed it at a few pages, and hoped it would work out the rest. It did not, because the answers a customer actually needs usually live somewhere else: in an inbox, a price list, a policy nobody published, or the head of the person who answers the phone.

A good assistant answers from content you have approved, says it is an assistant, and hands over to a person when it should. All three depend on a brief. Here is what goes in it.

1. The questions your customers really ask, with approved answers

Start with the questions, not the website. Go through the last few months of email, chat messages, contact-form enquiries and call notes, and write down every question a customer asked. Group the ones that mean the same thing. You will usually find that a small number of questions make up most of what people ask.

Then write the answer you would want a good member of staff to give, once, in plain words. This list becomes the assistant's core. Where an answer depends on the situation, say what it depends on, so the assistant can ask the right follow-up question instead of guessing.

  • Write answers the way you would say them, not the way a brochure would.
  • Mark any answer that changes often, such as opening hours or availability, so someone knows to keep it current.
  • Note the questions you do not want the assistant to answer at all.

2. What you sell, and how pricing works

Customers ask what you offer and what it costs before almost anything else. The assistant needs a clear description of each product or service: what it is, who it is for, what is included and what is not, and how long it takes.

Pricing needs a decision, not just a number. If your prices are fixed, give them. If every job is quoted, say so, and write the answer the assistant should give instead, for example what information it should collect so your team can quote. An assistant that invents a price because nobody told it what to say is worse than one that does not answer.

3. Your policies

Bookings, cancellations, refunds, delivery, warranties, payment terms, and how you handle personal information. These are the answers where a wrong reply costs money or trust, so they must come from the real policy, word for word where it matters.

If a policy only exists as habit, this is the moment to write it down. It helps your staff as much as the assistant.

4. How you sound

The assistant speaks for your brand, so it needs your voice. A short voice guide is enough: three or four words that describe how you sound, a few example replies you like, and a list of words and phrases you never use. Include how formal to be, whether to use first names, and which languages it should reply in. For many Philippine businesses that means deciding how to handle English, Filipino and Taglish.

If you already have brand guidelines, the tone section is a good starting point. If you do not, write examples rather than rules. An assistant copies examples far better than it follows adjectives.

5. When to hand over to a person

This is the document most teams forget, and the one that protects you. Write down:

  • What the assistant must never do. For example: confirm a price that is not on the approved list, give medical, legal or financial advice, or promise a date.
  • When it hands over. A complaint, a request it cannot answer from approved content, an upset customer, or anyone who asks for a person.
  • How it hands over. Who receives the conversation, through which channel, during which hours, and what the customer is told will happen next.
  • What it collects first. The details your team needs so the customer does not have to repeat themselves: name, contact details and what they need.

An assistant should also tell people it is an assistant. Customers are more forgiving of a clear handover than of a bot pretending to be a person.

Keep the brief alive

The brief is not finished at launch. Give each document an owner, and read the assistant's conversations regularly in the first weeks. Every question it could not answer is either a gap in the brief or a question it should hand over. Add the answer, or add the rule.

Before it goes live, test it with real questions from your inbox, including awkward ones. Have someone who did not write the brief try to make it say something it should not. Fix what they find, then launch.

What to do next

If you are planning an assistant, start with document one this week: collect the questions. It is useful even if you never build a chatbot, because it shows you what your website is failing to answer.
