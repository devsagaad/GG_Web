What a brand identity project includes

A logo is one part of a brand identity, and usually not the part that causes trouble later. What a full identity project delivers, and what it costs your business to skip each piece.

When a company asks for "a logo", it usually wants something bigger: to look as good as it is, everywhere it shows up. A logo alone cannot do that. A brand identity is the system that does: the mark, plus the rules and parts that let everyone on your team apply it the same way, on a website, a sign, a social post or a pitch deck.

Here is what a complete identity project includes, in the order it is usually built, and what happens when a piece is left out.

1. Strategy: what the brand has to do

Before anything is drawn, the studio should understand your market, your competitors and your customers, and agree with you in writing what the brand needs to achieve. Who is it for? What should people feel and believe when they see it? How is it different from the alternatives?

If you skip it: design reviews turn into debates about taste, because nobody agreed what the design is for. You end up choosing the option you like rather than the one that works.

2. Logomark and wordmark

The logomark is the symbol; the wordmark is your name set as a designed piece of type. A good identity designs them as one system, with versions that work at every size: large on a building, small as a website icon, in one colour on a receipt, and on dark and light backgrounds.

If you skip the system and keep one file: someone stretches it, recolours it or puts it on a background where it disappears, because there was no version made for that use.

3. Type system

The typefaces you use for headlines, body text and details, and the rules for combining them: sizes, weights, spacing. Typography carries most of what people actually read, so it shapes how your brand feels more than the logo does.

If you skip it: every document and page picks its own fonts, and the brand looks different everywhere it appears. It also matters for licensing: you need to know you are allowed to use the fonts on your website and in your materials.

4. Colour system

Not just a few brand colours, but a palette with roles: which colour is for backgrounds, which for text, which for buttons and highlights, and which combinations are readable. A good colour system is checked for contrast, so text stays legible for everyone, and works in print and on screen.

If you skip it: your brand colour appears in places it should not, text becomes hard to read, and print and screen versions drift apart.

5. Motion and 3D for the mark

More and more of where your brand appears moves: websites, social video, presentations, product launches. A modern identity includes how the mark animates, whether that is choppy 3D, flat 2D or a mix of both, so it arrives with the same character everywhere instead of whatever a video editor improvises.

If you skip it: each piece of motion is invented from scratch, by different people, and none of it feels like the same brand.

6. Guidelines your team can actually use

Guidelines turn the identity into something your staff and suppliers can apply without asking the designer every time. The most useful format is a working file, for example in Figma, that your team copies from directly, rather than a PDF that goes out of date the month it is sent.

If you skip it: the identity lives in one designer's head. When they are busy or gone, consistency goes with them.

7. Files you own

At handover you should receive every file, in every format you need, and clear ownership of the work. Ask about this before you start, not at the end.

If you skip it: you pay again every time you need a version of your own logo.

How long it takes

A brand identity with the parts above usually takes 4 to 6 weeks from kickoff: an audit, strategy agreed in writing, the identity reviewed with you every week, then motion and handover. Combined with a website, the whole project usually runs about 12 weeks.

What to ask a studio before you hire one

  • Which of the parts above are included, and in what form?
  • Is the strategy agreed in writing before design starts?
  • How often will you review the work, and with whom?
  • Do we own every file at the end, and in which formats?
  • Will the identity work on our website and in motion, not just in print?

Those are the questions we answer in every brand identity proposal.
